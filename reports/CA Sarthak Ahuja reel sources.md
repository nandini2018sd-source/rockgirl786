# Where CA Sarthak Ahuja's reels get their facts

*Research date: 22 Oct 2026. Account: [@casarthakahuja](https://www.instagram.com/casarthakahuja) (about 3.8–4M Instagram followers per [HypeAuditor](https://hypeauditor.com/instagram/casarthakahuja/)).*

## Method and limits

The research environment could not open Instagram, his website or his Substack directly. Network policy blocked every direct page fetch. So this report relies on:
- web-search snippets of his **Substack Notes**, where he reposts reel scripts word for word;
- his *Sunday Snippet* newsletter;
- interviews;
- news coverage of his reels.

For each claim, the report then searched for the original source. Most reels don't name a source, so the links between his claims and their sources are mostly **inferred**: the numbers or wording match, and the timing fits. These are marked "matches" rather than "cited".

## Short answer

His reels are built mainly from **primary and official material**, plus **news coverage of that material**:

1. **Government scheme documents and official programme pages**, Indian and foreign. This is his single biggest category: schemes, subsidies, visas, job programmes and tax rules.
2. **Academic studies**, usually picked up through press coverage of the paper.
3. **Market-research data** such as NielsenIQ, Kantar and Datum Intelligence, reached through business-media articles.
4. **Mainstream and trade business media**: ET, Mint, The Core, People Matters, Storyboard18 and similar.
5. **His own professional experience** as a CA, investment banker, virtual CFO and former private banker. This shapes the explanation and opinion layer rather than the facts.

There is **no evidence of a formal citation habit**. The reel scripts on Substack rarely name an outlet or link a source. When he does attribute, it is usually generic: "a study published in the Review of Economic Studies", "this scheme is called…".

## Patterns and reliability

- **What he covers:** opportunity-led explainers (schemes, grants, visas and foreign job programmes), business case studies, India macro, studies with a surprising twist, and wealth or community stories.
- **Accuracy pattern:** the underlying facts usually trace back to a real official or reputable source. The problems come from **simplification and framing in a 60-second format**:
  - an equity programme presented as a "grant" (Qatar);
  - an exchange target presented as "jobs" (Japan);
  - conditional tax rules presented broadly (ITCC, which drew a government fact-check).
- **Commercial content:** he runs brand campaigns, such as Hindware/Queo's #TwoVoicesOneUpgrade ([Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/brand-marketing/hindware-taps-gaurav-kapoor-sarthak-ahuja-for-twovoicesoneupgrade-creator-campaign-101899.htm)). These should be kept apart from his news and fact reels when judging his sourcing.
- **Regulatory scrutiny:** after the PIB episode, media coverage puts him within a wider SEBI/PIB crackdown on finfluencers ([Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/trending/from-sarthak-ahuja-to-avadhut-sathe-why-regulators-are-cracking-down-on-finfluencers-95657.htm)).

## How to verify any of his reels yourself

1. For a scheme or programme claim, find the official page (gov.in, PIB, the ministry or a foreign agency) and check amounts and eligibility. The headline figure is often the *maximum* available.
2. For a statistic, search the exact number in quotes. It usually leads to an ET, Mint, The Core or Inc42 article, which names the original data firm.
3. For a study, look up the paper title and journal and read the abstract. Check the sample (for example, the daughter study covered Danish small firms, not India).
4. For tax or legal claims, read the section text and check PIB Fact Check and Taxscan.
